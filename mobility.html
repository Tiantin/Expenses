Guide

Read once properly. Come back to the safety section whenever something starts hurting.

Your tilt is anterior

Hand slides through behind the lower back, belly pushes forward. That's a forward-rotated pelvis: front hip bones dropped, tailbone lifted, lumbar arch exaggerated.

It is not a flexibility problem. Most people stretch hip flexors for a month and nothing changes. It's a control problem — your ribs and pelvis have drifted apart, and your deep core, glutes and hamstrings aren't holding the pelvis in place under load.

The fix is roughly 70% strength and breathing, 30% length work. Stretch the hip flexors, absolutely. But the reps that change how you stand are the ones where you actively hold a posterior tilt against resistance — dead bugs, bridges, hollow holds, RKC planks.

Rib flare is your real cue

Forget the mirror. In every position in this program, the check is: are my lower ribs pulled down and knitted to my pelvis? If your ribs are flaring up and forward, the pelvis has tipped, no matter what your lower back feels like. Exhale fully — all the way to empty — and you'll feel the ribs drop. That's the position you're trying to own.

Static stretching before lifting

Long static holds right before heavy lifting temporarily reduce force output. Small effect, but you're chasing acceleration, so it counts. Static holds in this program are capped at 30–45 seconds. The long holds live in the evening block below — do those at night or on rest days, not twenty minutes before you squat.

Ankles and acceleration

Two separate things, both needed. Range — knee-over-toe dorsiflexion, so you can hit deep acceleration angles without the heel lifting or the knee caving in. And strength through that range — tibialis anterior, soleus, and the big toe. Range without strength doesn't make you faster, it just makes you bendy.

Your big toe extension is the most under-trained piece for sprinting. Every push-off finishes there. It's in the program daily for a reason.

Flow volume

Animal Flow doesn't progress by adding reps. It progresses by continuous time under tension, then complexity, then less rest. The rehearsal reps in Block 03 stay small on purpose — they exist so the shapes are fresh when you start the chain. Block 04 is the training.

  • Day 3 — learn the moves individually. No chain at all.
  • Day 9 — 2 rounds × 45 sec continuous, 60 sec rest.
  • Day 13 — 3 rounds × 60 sec, 45 sec rest.
  • Day 18 — 4 rounds × 60 sec, 30 sec rest. Add your own transitions.
  • Day 23 — 4 rounds × 90 sec, 30 sec rest, run reactively.
  • Day 27 — 5 rounds × 90 sec, 30 sec rest.
  • Day 30 — 3 free-form rounds, your own sequence.
There is a ceiling on how hard this session can go, because it runs before you lift. Flog the flow and your shoulders and core are cooked for pressing. If you want to train Animal Flow as a discipline rather than as preparation, that's a separate 45–60 minute session on a day you aren't pressing.

Safety

  • Never enter a deep ATG position cold. Block 01 exists for this.
  • Knees-over-toes work should feel like effort and stretch. Never sharp, never pinching. Pinching at the front of the ankle means elevate the heel slightly and cut the depth.
  • On heavy lower-body lift days, halve Block 02 so you don't arrive pre-fatigued.
  • Sore is fine. Painful is not. Genuine pain means stop that movement and see a physio in person — an app can't examine your joint.

Progression by week

  • Week 1 — learn the positions. Bodyweight only, no depth chasing. Halve the reps if needed. Feeling the right muscles beats getting sore.
  • Week 2 — full reps, 2-second pauses at end range, light load on split squats, calf raises and Copenhagens.
  • Week 3 — more depth, more load, longer flows, add the reactive work properly.
  • Week 4 — sport transfer. More speed and reactivity in Block 04, accelerations and bounds added on.

Evening decompression · 10 min

Optional, and separate from the pre-lift work. Do it on the nights you feel wound up. This is where deep tension actually leaves the body.

  • Legs up the wall, 4-in / 8-out breathing — 3 min
  • Couch stretch — 90 sec per side
  • 90/90 hip hold, folding over the front leg — 90 sec per side
  • Pancake fold — 90 sec
  • Thoracic extension over a roller — 1 min
  • Supine twist — 60 sec per side
  • Neck and jaw release — 1 min

After long days on site, the couch stretch, thoracic extension and legs-up-the-wall are the three that pay back most per minute.

What to expect

  • Week 1–2 — shin burn from tibialis raises, adductor soreness from Copenhagens, better ankle range within days. No postural change yet. That's normal.
  • Week 3–4 — deeper squat and split squat, more confident cutting and deceleration, the flow starts feeling continuous rather than like separate moves.
  • The tilt itself is a 3–6 month project, not a 30-day one. Thirty days buys you the control and the strength base. How you stand at rest follows several weeks behind that. Keep going.

After day 30

Run the calendar again with heavier loads and longer flow rounds, or drop to four days a week and put the freed time into sprint work and jumping. The sessions don't expire — the progressions do.
